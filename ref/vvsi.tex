\subsection{Context}
One of the biggest challenges in finance is to be able to accurately predict the prices of a asset, it being a stock paper, options, crypto currencies, commodities, any type of product offered in the market. The reason is simple: if you know the price of the next day, you are able to fully optimize your profit. The base idea on predicting the price is simple: we want to know the price in the future (one day, five days, ...) based on the historical data of that asset, which is characterized as a time-series. 

Time-series analysis is a complex type of investigation, since it involves many aspects such as stationarity, ergodicity, roughness, seasonality etc. Statistics have been tackling the problem of historical forecasting by analyzing these characteristics. However, with the rapid evolution of ML (Machine Learning) methods in the past 20 years, especially, DNN (Deep Neural Networks), with LSTMs and LLMs, there has been a risen on leveraging these models for performing such analysis, while delivering results that are better or even equal to statistical methods. 

\subsection{Motivations}
In the context of ML, the data is the key for a good model. However, the data is not explored as it's in the Statistics field, where the main focus is understanding properties of the data and devising a model dependent on those for forecasting the future and focused on one object of study (local model). The models in ML are built to derive the characteristics from the data and consequently perform the forecasting after a training process, while still maintaing the capability of being expanded for more than one subject (global model). 

In this study, we are interested in understading how well the base LSTM predicts the overnight return of stock papers, while being trained with data composed of multiple tickets. 

\subsection{Contributions}
In this project, we have two main contributions: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible pipeline for collecting and preparing 20 years of historical data across 1,834 equities.
    \item A global LSTM model that predicts next-day overnight returns across many companies.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Work}
Legacy econometric models as GARCH \cite{bollerslev1986} and SARIMA \cite{box2015} have been extensive used for the purpose of predicting equities prices due to their instrinsic capacity of accounting for data-specific properties such as seasonality, stationarity, mean-reversal, etc. However, they lack the necessary tools for explaining non-linear behaviour.

LSTMs \cite{vennerod2021} have been widely used as an substitute of RNNs due to its architecture amenizing the gradient-vanishing problem, while still maintaing an accurate cronological representation. Nevertheless, great majority of its implementations are with local models .

\section{Background}

\subsection{Financial Concepts}

Financial market data are commonly represented through OHLCV observations.
For each asset and trading day, \textit{Open} is the price at the beginning of
the trading session, \textit{High} and \textit{Low} are respectively the
highest and lowest prices observed during the session, and \textit{Close} is
the price at the end of the session. \textit{Volume} is the number of units
traded during that period. Together, these variables summarize the daily price
range, its endpoints, and the level of trading activity.

Prices from different assets are not directly comparable because their levels
and units differ. A return instead measures the relative change in price. The
simple close-to-close return for day $t$ is

\begin{equation}
    r_t = \frac{C_t-C_{t-1}}{C_{t-1}},
\end{equation}

where $C_t$ is the closing price on day $t$. A positive return represents a
price increase and a negative return represents a price decrease. Since
returns are relative quantities, they make movements across assets more
comparable than raw prices.

This project predicts the overnight return, defined as

\begin{equation}
    r_t^{\mathrm{overnight}}
    = \frac{O_t-C_{t-1}}{C_{t-1}},
\end{equation}

where $O_t$ is the opening price on day $t$. It measures the price movement
between the previous market close and the next market open. This interval can
reflect information released while the regular trading session is closed,
including company announcements, macroeconomic news, and developments in
other markets.

\subsection{Long Short-Term Memory Networks}

A Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is a recurrent neural network designed
to process ordered sequences while retaining information over time. At time
$t$, an LSTM receives the current input vector $x_t$, the previous hidden state
$h_{t-1}$, and the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$. Its behaviour is controlled
by gates whose values lie between zero and one.

The forget gate determines how much of the previous cell state is retained:

\begin{equation}
    f_t = \sigma\left(W_f[x_t,h_{t-1}] + b_f\right).
\end{equation}

The input gate determines how much new information is written, while the
candidate state contains the new information that may be stored:

\begin{align}
    i_t &= \sigma\left(W_i[x_t,h_{t-1}] + b_i\right), \\
    \widetilde{c}_t &= \tanh\left(W_c[x_t,h_{t-1}] + b_c\right).
\end{align}

The cell state is then updated by combining retained memory with new
information:

\begin{equation}
    c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \widetilde{c}_t,
\end{equation}

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. Finally, the output gate
selects which part of the cell state becomes the hidden state passed to the
next time step:

\begin{align}
    o_t &= \sigma\left(W_o[x_t,h_{t-1}] + b_o\right), \\
    h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t).
\end{align}

The main contribution of the LSTM architecture is its gated cell state. Its
additive update provides a more direct path through time for information and
gradients, reducing the vanishing-gradient problem found in conventional
recurrent neural networks. The model can therefore learn when to retain,
replace, or expose information and can represent dependencies over longer
periods. These properties make LSTMs suitable for financial time series, where
the relevance of past observations can vary over time. LSTMs do not eliminate
all optimization difficulties, but they make long-term dependencies
substantially easier to learn \cite{vennerod2021}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $y_i$ be the observed return, $\hat{y}_i$ the predicted return,
$\bar{y}$ the mean observed return, and $n$ the number of predictions. This
project reports five complementary evaluation metrics.

The Mean Squared Error (MSE) is

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2.
\end{equation}

MSE is the optimization loss used to train the model. Squaring the errors
penalizes large mistakes more strongly than small ones. Lower values are
better, although the result is expressed in squared-return units.

The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}.
\end{equation}

RMSE retains the stronger penalty for large errors but is expressed in the
same units as the predicted return, making it easier to interpret than MSE.

The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i-\hat{y}_i|.
\end{equation}

MAE represents the average absolute prediction error in return units. It gives
each error a linear weight and is therefore less sensitive to isolated large
errors than MSE and RMSE.

The symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE) is

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{sMAPE}
    = \frac{200}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
      \frac{|y_i-\hat{y}_i|}{|y_i|+|\hat{y}_i|+\varepsilon},
\end{equation}

where $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. sMAPE expresses error
as a percentage and is bounded between 0\% and 200\%, with lower values being
better. It is used instead of MAPE because financial returns frequently equal
or approach zero and can be negative; dividing only by the observed value can
therefore produce extremely large or undefined MAPE values. Despite its
symmetry, sMAPE can still assign a large percentage error when both returns are
close to zero, so it should be interpreted together with the absolute-error
metrics.

The coefficient of determination ($R^2$) is

\begin{equation}
    R^2 = 1-
    \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}
         {\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}.
\end{equation}

An $R^2$ of 1 represents perfect predictions, while 0 indicates performance
equivalent to always predicting the mean observed return. A negative value
means that the model performs worse than that mean baseline. Unlike the four
error metrics, a higher $R^2$ is better.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}
Data processing was a central part of the pipeline.

The \textbf{first step} collected 5,984 NASDAQ and NYSE ticker symbols. Their
different share-class representations were filtered, deduplicated, and
normalized to the hyphenated format expected by Yahoo Finance.

The \textbf{second step} downloaded the maximum available daily OHLCV history
through YFinance. A rate-aware scheduler checkpointed each request and retried
rate limits and transient failures, producing usable histories for 5,842
tickers. Automatic price adjustment made historical OHLC prices consistent
after splits and dividends, avoiding artificial jumps in the return series.

The \textbf{third step} selected the 1,834 tickers with complete, non-missing
OHLCV coverage from January 1, 2006 to January 1, 2026. This universe contains
9,226,853 daily observations before constructing returns and rolling windows.

The \textbf{fourth and final step} derived the relative daily range
$(H_t-L_t)/C_t$, log-volume $\ln(V_t)$, overnight return
$(O_t-C_{t-1})/C_{t-1}$, and intraday return $(C_t-O_t)/O_t$. Log-volume was
standardized per ticker using training-only statistics, and the next-day
overnight return was used as the target. The chronological split uses
2006--2015 for training, 2016--2018 for validation, and 2019--2025 for testing.

\subsection{Model}
The model uses a custom PyTorch LSTM cell instead of the built-in
\texttt{nn.LSTM}, providing explicit control over its gates and initialization.
It has four input features, one recurrent layer with a 64-dimensional hidden
and cell state, and a linear output layer that predicts one overnight-return
value from the final hidden state. Gate and output weights use Xavier uniform
initialization, while the forget-gate bias is initialized to one to encourage
the retention of earlier information at the start of training. Three otherwise
identical models use window sizes of 10, 30, and 100 trading days to evaluate
the effect of different historical horizons. We use MSE as the ADAM optimizer metric. 

\section{Implementation}
The system trains the global LSTM on the aggregated multi-ticker dataset. At
each epoch, the training and validation metrics are logged to Weights \& Biases
(W\&B), allowing their evolution to be monitored throughout training. Whenever
the validation MSE improves, the current checkpoint replaces the previous one,
so only the best model is retained. After training, this checkpoint is reloaded
and evaluated on the training, validation, and test sets. The final metrics are
presented in a summary table, together with the 15 validation and test tickers
with the lowest MSE for a per-ticker comparison.
